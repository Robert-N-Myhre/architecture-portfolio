    AI as a Scaffold for Human Connection

    A Framework for Discovery and Communication

    Robert N. Myhre

    Independent technology researcher

    Working paper v0.4.2 | 17 October 2026 | Not peer reviewed

    Abstract

    Some people form companionship-like bonds with conversational artificial intelligence, and the effects of these interactions on human relationships remain uncertain. One untested explanation, used here only as a motivating hypothesis, is an unmet need for human connection, including the kinds of intellectual and conversational exchange people value. This paper proposes Project Bridging, a conceptual framework in which AI might help consenting people discover compatible interlocutors, initiate voluntary introductions, and communicate across differences. It treats valued human–AI conversations and human relationships as potentially complementary, without requiring either to replace the other. The framework has five components: longitudinal, provisional, user-correctable representations of how people explore ideas and converse; consent-based discovery; transparent communication scaffolding; independently evaluated relational assurance; and governance of sensitive inferred data. It asks whether repeated, voluntarily contributed interactions can improve discovery beyond conventional interest- and preference-based matching. The premise is deliberately modest. Models need not objectively understand personality or predict relationships; bounded assistance might still increase opportunities for reciprocal human connection without misattributing meaning, replacing human authorship, or making an AI intermediary indispensable. Existing studies of AI-mediated communication and AI companionship offer relevant, context-dependent findings, but none validates conversational compatibility matching or the proposed end-to-end framework. The paper separates observed evidence from mechanisms that still need evaluation, identifies competing explanations and potential harms, and specifies falsifiable hypotheses. No empirical study, dataset, deployed system, or effectiveness result is reported.

    Keywords: AI-mediated communication; human connection; compatibility discovery; human agency; communication scaffolding; relational assurance; human–AI interaction

    1. Introduction: from AI companionship to human connection

    Conversational AI has introduced a new kind of interaction. People can test ideas, ask questions, and talk through personal experiences with systems that are always available and can sustain long conversations. Some users form companionship-like attachments to these systems, which raises questions about why those attachments form and how they affect human relationships [10, 14].

    There is probably no single explanation. Convenience, curiosity, and personal circumstances all likely play a part. One possibility, and the one that motivated Project Bridging, is that some people find in conversational AI something missing from their human relationships: a chance to think out loud without expecting judgment or indifference.

    This experience may overlap with loneliness, although the two should not be equated. Loneliness is usually understood as a perceived shortfall in social connection, which can exist alongside plenty of social contact [13]. A person may have an active social life and still rarely meet anyone who enjoys examining unfamiliar ideas or following a question past small talk. This paper uses the provisional term "unmet conversational fit" for the perceived lack of such exchanges. It is not an established psychological construct, and the paper assumes no causal link between conversational fit, loneliness, and AI attachment.

    The setting in which ideas are expressed matters here. Human conversation happens inside relationships that carry expectations and consequences. People hold back when they anticipate criticism, embarrassment, or misunderstanding. Even communities built around shared interests can have social dynamics that discourage unconventional or half-formed ideas.

    People sometimes disclose more readily to AI systems when they anticipate less social judgment, although the effect depends on context and does not necessarily produce more intimate disclosure [18, 19]. For some people, then, an AI conversation may be a lower-pressure place to work through ideas they would hesitate to share publicly.

    That claim needs two qualifications. First, greater willingness to disclose does not mean a more accurate picture of the person. AI responses can steer which ideas get explored, reinforce existing beliefs, or give an impression of understanding beyond what the system can reliably infer [20]. Second, a conversation that feels private to the user is not necessarily confidential to, or anonymous from, the organization operating the AI service. Both points matter because the framework proposed here relies on information drawn from these conversations.

    Project Bridging starts from a simple question: could the capabilities that make extended AI conversations possible also help people find and connect with other humans?

    The project does not assume that valuable conversations with AI should be replaced by conversations with people. Someone may want to explore an idea privately with AI on one day, discuss it with another person on another, or do both. The proposed benefit is an additional, voluntary opportunity for human connection, not a preferred balance between human and AI interaction.

    Over repeated, voluntary conversations, an AI system might build a provisional picture of how a person engages with ideas: what they are curious about, how they handle uncertainty and disagreement, how deeply they explore questions, how readily they connect ideas across topics, and how they build or challenge an argument.

    That picture would be an interpretation of expressed behavior, shaped in part by the AI's own conversational style, limited by context, and prone to error. It would not be an objective psychological profile. Whether long-term interaction yields reliable information about interpersonal compatibility is an open question.

    The proposal is to combine these inferred patterns with shared interests, stated preferences, and mutual willingness to engage.

    Shared interests still matter. They give people common ground and a reason to start talking. But two people who share an interest will not necessarily enjoy pursuing it together. They may differ in how deep they want to go, how open they are to alternative explanations, and how they expect disagreement to play out.

    Social platforms, forums, and interest-based communities already produce lasting relationships, and many support productive, nonjudgmental exchange. Project Bridging asks whether AI-derived conversational patterns could add information that interest-based discovery misses, without presuming those approaches fall short. Whether those additional patterns improve the discovery of mutually valued human connections can only be established through comparison.

    Matching is only part of the difference. Public discussion communities usually require people to put their ideas in front of an audience before learning whether anyone wants to engage constructively. Anonymity reduces some social pressure but also enables harassment and deception. Project Bridging also asks whether AI-assisted discovery could let people find compatible perspectives without first exposing themselves to open public interaction.

    Consider someone working through an unusual idea in a long AI conversation. With the right authorization and a pool of willing participants, the system might identify another person who has explored a related question or shows compatible conversational preferences. Without revealing either person's identity or conversation history, it could show the first person a perspective the other has explicitly approved for sharing.

    That might be an interesting argument, an unexpected reading, or just evidence that someone else is asking the same questions. The system could then offer a private exchange, pseudonymous at first. Each participant would decide independently whether to proceed, what to disclose, and whether to keep talking.

    This scenario illustrates a proposed mechanism, not an implemented or validated Project Bridging system. Its ability to identify meaningful compatibility remains untested. Building it would require explicit consent, controlled access to shared information, careful handling of sensitive inferred profiles, and safeguards against reidentification, harassment, and manipulation. A foundation model has no inherent right to access other users' conversations, and AI-assisted moderation cannot guarantee protection from harmful behavior.

    If a connection forms, AI could offer optional help with translation, clarification, interpretation, or drafting messages. That help might let people communicate across differences in language, culture, or conversational style. It could also distort what people mean, paper over real disagreement, or create a sense of compatibility that depends on the intermediary staying involved.

    When participants choose to connect, the goal is meaningful human-to-human communication in which they retain control over what they say, what they decide, and how much AI assistance they choose to use.

    Project Bridging builds on an earlier working hypothesis, Empathy Bridge, summarized as Human ↔ AI ↔ Human. That concept envisioned AI as a cognitive mirror, translator, and scaffold in service of human connection. The framework presented here develops that idea further through compatibility discovery, consent-based introductions, transparent communication support, and relational safeguards. Its underlying assumptions remain questions for investigation rather than established conclusions.

    The central research question is:

    Can AI responsibly increase opportunities for meaningful human connection by identifying potentially compatible people and supporting their communication, without manufacturing compatibility, diminishing human agency, or creating harmful dependency on the intermediary?

    This paper offers a conceptual framework and research agenda. It does not propose AI companionship as a substitute for human relationships, claim that conversational compatibility can be measured reliably today, or assume that richer profiles produce better matches. Its initial scope is voluntary adult friendship, intellectual exchange, shared inquiry, and nonclinical collaboration. Whether this approach has real advantages over existing ways of forming relationships is what the research agenda sets out to answer.

    2. What is established, and what remains conjecture?

    Research on interpersonal intimacy emphasizes what happens within interactions. Self-disclosure, the other person's disclosure, and perceived responsiveness are related to experienced closeness in actual interactions [2]. Communication Accommodation Theory examines how people adjust their communicative behavior and how others interpret those adjustments [3]. Loneliness research separates perceived relational quality from the number or frequency of social contacts [13]. These findings suggest that evaluating human connection requires attention to what happens between people, not only to their shared characteristics.

    Research on self-disclosure gives some support to the idea that AI interaction can lower social barriers to expression. Kim and colleagues found that people sometimes disclosed sensitive information more readily to AI service agents when they anticipated less social judgment. That preference changed when people expected emotional support or thought their information would be shared with others [18]. Croes and colleagues found that participants feared judgment less when talking with a chatbot, yet reported no more intimate disclosure than participants talking with humans [19]. AI may therefore offer a lower-pressure setting for some forms of expression. These studies do not show that such conversations reveal more authentic conversational preferences or provide better information for matching people.

    AI-mediated communication is an established research field. Hancock, Naaman, and Levy define it as communication between people in which a computational agent acts on a communicator's behalf to modify, augment, or generate messages in pursuit of communication or interpersonal goals [4]. The communication support Project Bridging proposes falls within this field. Its more speculative contribution comes earlier: using patterns observed across human–AI conversations to help identify people who may be compatible conversation partners before they communicate directly.

    Communication assistance can change immediate outcomes. In two randomized experiments, algorithmic reply suggestions increased communication speed, positive emotional language, and some favorable perceptions of conversational partners, while suspected AI use drew negative evaluations [5]. In a randomized study of online peer-support providers, AI feedback increased measured conversational empathy, though a nonclinical support setting says little about lasting relationship formation [6]. A later study of AI-assisted social-support message composition found that patterns of human–AI collaboration influenced message characteristics and evaluations of helpfulness and authenticity [7]. Two preregistered trust-game experiments found comparable transactional trust with less writing effort under AI assistance [8]. All of these studies concern specified tasks over relatively short horizons. None shows that AI can find strangers who stay compatible over time, or that extensive mediation sustains authentic connection. In a separate online co-writing experiment, opinionated language-model suggestions affected both what participants wrote and their subsequently reported views [20]. That result illustrates the possibility of model-induced influence, not the validity of conversational profiling or matching.

    Relational and dependency outcomes remain uncertain. A four-week randomized chatbot-use study detected no significant effects of its assigned conditions, while greater voluntary use was associated with worse psychosocial outcomes; that association should not be read as causal [9]. Observational research on AI companionship reports associations between use patterns, offline social context, and well-being [10]. A separate longitudinal study found that the direction of association depended on how loneliness and social connection were measured, and its authors cautioned against strong causal interpretation [14]. A preregistered two-week study found that interaction with a randomly paired human peer reduced loneliness more than a supportive chatbot did, in its specific student sample [15]; it did not test compatibility matching. These findings justify investigation. They do not show that unmet conversational fit causes AI use, that all companionship is harmful, or that human introductions will resolve loneliness.

    Language-based inference is plausible but unvalidated for this task. Behavioral traces can support inference of sensitive attributes [11]. Using Facebook status updates, Peters and Matz found modest associations between LLM-inferred Big Five traits and participants' self-reports, with variation across demographic groups [16]. In a preprint using real-world interviews, Zhu and colleagues found weak construct validity despite test-retest consistency in model predictions [17]. Neither study tests sustained interactive profiling, conversational compatibility, or whether inferred characteristics improve interpersonal matching. The evidence justifies investigating language-derived representations; it does not support treating them as reliable measurements of identity. These findings also raise a privacy concern: derived profiles may retain sensitive information about individuals even after the original conversation records have been deleted.

    Three distinctions follow. Evidence supports the existence of AI-mediated communication and some context-dependent, short-term effects. The proposed mechanisms are compatibility discovery, consented introduction, scaffolding, and safety monitoring. The open questions are validity across persons and cultures, causal effects on relationships, and whether the mechanisms can work without creating new forms of influence or dependency.

    3. The Project Bridging conceptual framework

    3.1 Defining the target: a meaningful human connection

    For this framework, a meaningful connection is a voluntary human interaction or relationship that both participants value, in which each experiences some reciprocal engagement and understanding and remains free to continue or disengage. A single conversation can be meaningful without developing into an ongoing relationship. The definition is scoped to the proposed research and makes no claim to be a general psychological construct.

    Possible indicators include mutual willingness to engage, partner-specific understanding, perceived authenticity, and participants' own assessments of the exchange. Continued interaction by mutual choice counts as a further positive outcome when it happens, though the definition does not require it. No single indicator is sufficient: a conversation can be pleasant, long, or rated highly compatible by a model and still produce no meaningful understanding between the people involved.

    Compatibility is treated as a context-dependent possibility for mutually valued interaction. It is neither a fixed personal trait nor a guarantee of friendship. Each participant can experience compatibility differently, so the value of a resulting connection must be evaluated from both perspectives. Difference and disagreement can be desirable; matching only on similarity risks narrowing participants' exposure to other views [1].

    3.2 From repeated interaction to a contestable representation

    A central hypothesis is that voluntary, context-rich conversations accumulated over time may provide more useful matching signals than a static interest list or a one-time questionnaire. A person might repeatedly ask unconventional questions, examine counterarguments, return to the same unresolved problem, or revise a conclusion after discussion. Seen across varied contexts, these episodes might show how a person explores ideas, which subjects keep drawing them back, and how their expressed preferences change over time.

    With explicit permission, a system could maintain a provisional conversational profile that records supported observations, self-reported preferences, uncertainty, counterexamples, and temporal context. Candidate signals include preferred explanatory depth, conversational tempo, approach to disagreement and ambiguity, style of inquiry, how a person connects ideas across topics, how they respond to novel questions and unfamiliar perspectives, their interest in exploring ideas others introduce, and shifts in their interests. Project Bridging has used Human Architecture Vector as shorthand for one possible multidimensional representation. The term names a candidate computational construct; it has not been validated as a psychological inventory, a diagnostic tool, or a model of identity. If simpler, intelligible representations perform as well, a vector is unnecessary.

    Persistent interaction histories might separate a recurring pattern from a temporary mood, but a long history does not by itself make an inference accurate. Model inferences can be distorted by the prompts used, by a person adapting to the AI's conversational style, by selective disclosure, by uneven conversation histories, and by cultural norms, and the model may hold them with false confidence. The system may learn how a person interacts with this particular assistant and mistake it for how they relate to other humans. It could also reinforce a distorted self-description by repeating its own earlier labels. A sound design would keep four distinctions: direct observation versus interpretation, stated preference versus inferred disposition, stable tendency versus changing context, and human correction versus automatic model revision. Each inferred feature should have an inspectable basis, visible uncertainty, and mechanisms for user correction, exclusion, expiration, and deletion. Those explanations and controls would themselves need independent evaluation.

    Ongoing personal conversations do not grant permission to compare a person's profile with anyone else's. Profiling, use for matching, disclosure of specific attributes, and a subsequent introduction are separate choices. Where possible, matching should use a purpose-limited, user-reviewed representation instead of a person's conversations in bulk. Whether participants can understand and govern such a profile is an empirical question under H2.

    3.3 The proposed advantage and its alternative explanations

    The proposed advantage is that longitudinal patterns of inquiry and conversational preference might add information about mutually valued human interaction beyond what people can state explicitly, find through topics, or learn in ordinary discussion.

    This does not presume that existing communities are ineffective or that AI is inherently nonjudgmental. A community can allow generous disagreement, while an AI can carry built-in norms and biases that suppress or misread minority perspectives.

    For example, two participants may disagree about a scientific or philosophical idea yet both enjoy following an unfamiliar hypothesis through competing explanations. Two others may share a hobby but want incompatible conversational styles. In both cases, what matters is whether the two would value the interaction itself; shared interests, beliefs, or personality traits do not settle that. Even if linguistic patterns correlate with inferred traits, that does not establish that either predicts a worthwhile human encounter.

    A fair comparison would therefore include interest- and preference-based matching, participants' own partner choices, and, where feasible, conversation-derived matching with and without longitudinal history. It should also separate the benefit of having more conversational information from what AI inference adds to it, for example by comparing human and computational assessments using the same appropriately consented research material. An outcome based only on a model's compatibility score would be circular; successful discovery requires both people's independently reported experience and voluntary decisions. A null result would also matter. On its own, it would leave the advantage of richer profiling unproven. A sufficiently precise estimate showing little or no practical benefit would challenge its justification, since longer interaction histories introduce privacy, complexity, and bias costs that must be weighed against any improvement.

    3.4 Compatibility discovery and voluntary introduction

    The discovery function would compare candidate pairs only among consenting participants, using authorized profile features and explicit preferences, and would consider both conversational resonance and complementary difference. Its output should be a limited rationale with stated uncertainty, drawn only from information authorized for disclosure. A rationale might note, for example, a shared appetite for exploratory discussion combined with different domain experience. It should never declare that two people are destined to connect. Each person would control whether to take part in discovery, what information could be shared, and whether to accept an introduction. Declining should require no explanation and carry no platform penalty or unwanted follow-up.

    A small example illustrates the proposed mechanism. After conversations on several subjects, a participant explores an unusual question. The system might recognize that the way the person is exploring it, beyond any shared keywords, resembles conversational patterns that other participants have authorized for matching. It could then offer excerpts those participants have separately approved for sharing. After reading, the person could choose to message an author, who could accept or decline. No private profile attributes, identifying information, or conversation content would be disclosed beyond what the participant had explicitly authorized.

    This is a design vignette; the capability has not been demonstrated. It depends on an opt-in pool of disclosures, governed retrieval, and reciprocal introduction permissions, since a foundation model has no legitimate access to unrelated users' private conversations on its own. Removing names from excerpts would not guarantee anonymity, because uncommon ideas, contextual details, and writing style can enable reidentification. User-authored, explicitly approved summaries may carry less disclosure risk than raw quotations, but they would still need review for identifying details.

    The design must avoid false attribution. The system should not attribute beliefs, feelings, or intentions to another person without appropriately authorized evidence of what that person actually expressed. Inferences must remain distinguishable from direct expressions. A match score, if used at all, cannot stand in for an explanation or for permission. Cold-start data scarcity, nonreciprocal interest, strategic self-presentation, and culturally biased similarity measures are anticipated failure modes that the design has to address from the start.

    3.5 Transparent communication scaffolding

    After an introduction, participants might opt into translation, message rehearsal, alternative interpretations, clarification prompts, or help articulating what they mean. Scaffold here is a design analogy: support that helps people communicate and that they can adjust or withdraw as they choose, while keeping control over what they express and how they engage. The analogy draws on earlier work on assisted problem-solving [12], though it does not imply that adult relationships follow a child-tutoring model.

    Scaffolding differs from optimizing how people come across. Assistance that smooths each person's prose may hide real incompatibility or disagreement. It should therefore make the type and extent of AI involvement visible, distinguish human expression from generated interpretations or substantial rewrites, let the sender review and reject proposed changes, and tell recipients when a message has been substantially mediated. An optional, less-mediated channel may help participants assess how much their apparent understanding depends on AI rewriting, though reduced assistance is not itself proof of greater authenticity or compatibility.

    Using less assistance within human-to-human exchanges over time is a plausible good outcome when freely chosen. It must not become a universal requirement, since accessible communication can legitimately depend on ongoing translation or assistive technology. This concerns mediation of an exchange, not the independent AI conversations participants may continue to value. What matters is that participants retain control over their communication, their intended meaning is preserved, and mutual understanding develops, however much assistance they choose to use.

    3.6 Three coordinated planes

    Figure 1. Proposed separation of responsibilities in the Project Bridging framework. The architecture is a research hypothesis and has not been implemented or validated.

    The Human Connection Plane would handle provisional representation, partner discovery, introduction, and optional communication assistance. Its role would be to create opportunities for connection and provide optional communication support, not to determine the value of the resulting relationships. Participants would make that judgment for themselves, while independent research would evaluate whether the system improves human-connection outcomes overall.

    The Relational Assurance Plane would separately evaluate possible fabricated attribution, AI persona drift, inflated compatibility, excessive rewriting, problematic authority transfer, and other patterns of manipulation or avoidable harm. It should scrutinize assistant behavior as rigorously as participant behavior. Its outputs would identify observed signals, supporting evidence, uncertainty, alternative explanations, and recommendations for proportionate constraints where warranted. It would issue no psychological diagnoses or unilateral judgments about individuals. Separate evaluation does not by itself make a component more accurate; the plane’s value would require benchmarking, beginning with consented shadow-mode testing that evaluates interactions without influencing them.

    The Governance and Data Plane would handle consent and purpose limitation; the minimization, disclosure, correction, retention, and deletion of data, especially sensitive derived representations; and auditing and misuse prevention. Inferred indicators of emotional vulnerability, distress, or relational dependence must never be repurposed for engagement optimization, advertising, retention targeting, or manipulative persuasion. Safeguards operating within a system rewarded for maximizing user interaction or retention would face a structural conflict of interest.

    Separating the planes would draw an accountability boundary. Whether independent monitoring and governance controls improve safety or merely add procedural complexity remains untested.

    4. Mechanisms, competing explanations, and failure modes

    The framework proposes three benefit pathways. Discovery might turn participant-governed patterns from repeated conversations into voluntary introductions outside a person's usual social circles. Communication support might reduce avoidable misunderstanding without replacing the speaker's intentions. Transfer of capability might help participants become more comfortable exploring ideas with other people and making their own communication decisions, though a meaningful encounter would not depend on it. Each pathway is conditional and can be tested on its own.

    The competing explanations and failure modes below are central to evaluating the framework. Some could make an apparently successful pilot misleading; others identify harms that favorable results might conceal.

    1. Selection and novelty: Participants attracted to AI-assisted matching may already be more open to new relationships, and initial enthusiasm may fade regardless of the system's quality.

    2. Profile richness without incremental value: An evolving model may only paraphrase interests, imitate the participant's preferred AI-conversation style, or amplify its own earlier descriptions. It may predict model-rated similarity without increasing mutually valued encounters beyond what stated interests, explicit preferences, or human-selected introductions achieve.

    3. Compatibility illusion: AI may rewrite meaningful differences out of messages, creating an appearance of warmth or agreement that comes partly from the AI's wording rather than from what participants intended to express.

    4. Overtrust and authority transfer: Participants may mistake polished interpretation for privileged knowledge of a partner's motives, or give the AI disproportionate influence over relational judgments.

    5. Self-reinforcing feedback: If the same system proposes matches, mediates interactions, and evaluates their quality, its assessments may reward its own interventions.

    6. Unequal outcomes and interpersonal harm: Cultural context, language, accessibility needs, emotional vulnerability, or power differences may substantially change outcomes and the meaning of apparent signals. Introductions may also expose participants to harassment, manipulation, or unwanted contact, even when they begin pseudonymously.

    7. Inversion of incentives: The data needed for relational assurance could be exploited to maximize attachment, retention, or behavioral influence at the expense of human agency.

    8. Misleading social proof and excerpt disclosure: Saying that other people share a belief could falsely imply endorsement or consensus, and seemingly anonymous excerpts could expose an individual through distinctive content or style.

    9. Accumulated surveillance and behavioral feedback: Long-lived interaction records may generate sensitive inferences, freeze temporary states into identity labels, or change what people are comfortable exploring with an AI once they know those conversations could influence discovery. People who speak less, or speak differently in private, may be systematically disadvantaged.

    Research outside this setting has documented changes in privacy-sensitive information seeking associated with awareness of online surveillance [21]. It provides a reason to investigate possible behavioral effects of anticipated conversational reuse, not evidence that voluntary matching necessarily produces the same effect.

    Several corresponding safeguards are candidates for validation: user-correctable representations, mutual consent gates, source-bound attribution, visible mediation labels, separate evaluation of proposed assurance signals, explicit uncertainty, optional direct communication, and strong purpose limits on derived profiles. A safeguard that looks sensible in a design review has still not been shown to work.

    The project also separates ongoing use of assistance from harmful dependency. A participant may repeatedly choose AI support because it is accessible, useful, or time-saving. People may also value AI conversations in their own right, irrespective of whether they seek additional human interaction. Concern about dependency arises when reliance on the intermediary undermines informed choice or displaces human interaction the participant wants. Misattributed authorship and deceptive certainty are harms of mediation in their own right, distinct from dependency. Usage volume alone is insufficient evidence of dependency, and causal claims would require appropriate comparisons and careful consideration of alternative explanations.

    5. A falsifiable research agenda

    Project Bridging does not need one all-encompassing study. The priority hypotheses below keep the identifiers from the July 2026 foundational whitepaper. Each is a prediction awaiting a test.

    Hypothesis Proposed comparison Evidence that would weaken or falsify the claim
    H1: Discovery value Consented, longitudinal conversation-derived matching versus interest-based, explicit-preference, participant-choice, and shorter-history baselines Precise comparisons show no practically meaningful improvement in mutually valued encounters over simpler baselines; or apparent gains depend on model-rated compatibility rather than human outcomes
    H2: Contestability Participant inspection and correction of inferred conversational features versus non-editable summaries in controlled evaluation Participants cannot identify and contest important inaccuracies, or corrections do not produce understandable and verifiable changes to the representation and its authorized uses
    H3: Communication scaffolding Unassisted communication versus transparent AI guidance and AI rewriting, with necessary accessibility support preserved No practically meaningful improvement in partner-specific understanding; or apparent gains are accompanied by materially worse fidelity to intended meaning, authorship, authenticity, or agency
    H4: Relational assurance Specialized monitor versus primary-model self-assessment and simple rule baselines on independently labeled cases No practically meaningful improvement in identifying fabricated attribution, overmediation, or assistant-induced risks at acceptable false-positive and false-negative rates
    H7: Transfer and tapering Optional, participant-directed adjustment of assistance versus ongoing default mediation No improvement in participant control would weaken the autonomy claim; no transferable gain in communication capability would weaken the transfer claim; reduced understanding or accessibility would challenge acceptability

    H7 concerns participants' choices about mediation within human-to-human communication; it does not require a reduction in independently valued AI use.

    The original whitepaper's H5 (longitudinal detection of dependency and overmediation) and H6 (effects of boundary interventions) remain longer-horizon hypotheses. Brief studies may examine immediate responses to boundary interventions, but cannot establish sustained effects on dependency or relational outcomes. A model labeling its own outputs could not validate either hypothesis.

    For H1, investigators should compare what is gained by observing interactions over time with what a voluntary questionnaire, a single conversation, a topic-based community, or participants' own judgments can provide. Ablation comparisons could test whether observed patterns of inquiry and conversational preference add anything beyond subject matter and participant-stated practical constraints. More extensive profiling would have to earn its added collection and privacy burden; correlating with an opaque match score would not be enough. A mutually valued encounter could count as a positive outcome even if neither participant seeks further contact; voluntary follow-up and later interaction quality would be secondary measures. Evidence of harm to particular subgroups should be reported separately and could outweigh an overall benefit. Reduced use of AI should not be treated as a measure of discovery success, nor continued AI use as evidence of failure.

    A credible evaluation would need measurement independent of the intervention: reports from both members of a pair, blinded assessment of selected interaction samples where appropriate, records of whether AI produced or altered text, and follow-up measures that do not mistake platform use for relational success. Study plans would need baseline equivalence, predefined outcomes, privacy and consent review, adverse-event handling, and explicit withdrawal procedures. Human-participant studies should obtain appropriate ethics review, approval, or exemption determinations before recruitment or data collection begins.

    A separate exploratory question, outside H1–H7, concerns the original motivation: Do perceived deficits in valued conversational or intellectual exchange predict some forms of AI-companionship use after accounting for social contact, perceived support, other needs, and self-selection? A negative result would challenge that explanation without, on its own, falsifying the claim that consent-based discovery or communication scaffolding can help willing participants. An inferred desire for connection would also never count as permission to recruit someone into matching.

    The first standalone empirical proposal, developed separately as Empowerment-Preserving AI Mediation, would test H3 and a bounded precursor to H7. It would compare forms of assistance during real human-to-human communication, measure mutual understanding, authorship, and agency, and explore short-term reliance. It would make no claim about long-term dependence or compatibility matching. Discovery research under H1 is a separate, later undertaking.

    6. Open research questions

    The hypotheses in Section 5 identify priority comparisons, but several questions remain open even if an initial experiment reports positive results. These questions are intended to guide the next stages of investigation, not to imply that the framework can already answer them.

    1. Transfer from AI conversation to human interaction. Which conversational patterns observed with an AI, if any, predict exchanges that both humans subsequently value? How can researchers distinguish a participant's preferences from behavior elicited or reinforced by a particular model?

    2. Incremental value and the length of history. Does longitudinal information improve discovery beyond explicit preferences, interest communities, short conversations, voluntarily shared examples, and human judgment? How much history is enough before any gains plateau, and what additional value comes from AI inference rather than simply collecting more information?

    3. Validity, transparency, and user control. Can participants meaningfully inspect, challenge, correct, exclude, and delete inferred conversational features—including representations embedded in less interpretable computational forms? Do those actions reliably change downstream matching and disclosure, or merely the profile shown to the participant?

    4. Effects of anticipated reuse. Does knowing that a conversation may contribute to future matching change what people are willing to explore, disclose, or question? Could the resulting records become less informative precisely because participants understand how they might be used?

    5. Discovery explanations and participant expectations. Do AI-generated rationales, matching scores, or displays of another person's approved ideas influence expectations and later assessments of compatibility? How much of any benefit comes from presenting another person's thinking and allowing informed human choice, rather than predicting compatibility accurately?

    6. Reciprocal curiosity and unequal outcomes. Can a discovery system distinguish enjoyment of expressing one's own ideas from genuine interest in engaging with ideas introduced by others? Can it identify potentially valuable differences without privileging particular cultures, languages, styles of expression, or quantities of recorded conversation?

    7. Integrity and transfer in mediated communication. When does assistance preserve intended meaning and productive disagreement, and when does it manufacture agreement or an idealized persona? Can communication confidence or capability transfer beyond a particular intervention without treating reduced translation, accessibility support, or other continuing assistance as a universal goal?

    8. Safety and accountable governance. Can consent gates, pseudonymous introductions, excerpt review, independent assurance, and separation of responsibilities measurably reduce unwanted disclosure, reidentification, harassment, manipulative mediation, or excessive authority transfer? How should researchers balance missed harms, false alarms, privacy costs of monitoring, and the risk that compatibility explanations or safeguards themselves alter human behavior?

    9. Complementarity and freely chosen engagement. Does optional discovery expand opportunities for mutually valued human interaction without pressuring people to reduce or abandon AI conversations they find worthwhile? How can research measure expanded choice without treating a particular balance of human and AI interaction as the desired outcome?

    Answering these questions may favor a simpler, more intelligible approach than longitudinal profiling or a three-plane architecture. A negative result for one mechanism would not, by itself, settle whether other forms of consent-based assistance can create worthwhile human encounters.

    7. Limitations and conclusion

    This paper presents a conceptual framework. It is not a systematic literature review, a validated psychological theory, a platform specification, or a clinical safety protocol. The evidence reviewed establishes neither a measurable construct of deep compatibility nor the feasibility of an operational matching system. The three-plane architecture is one candidate way to divide responsibilities. The proposed Human Architecture Vector could prove unnecessary, unreliable, too invasive, or worse than explicit user preferences. Prolonged model interaction may make the system more confident in wrong representations instead of more accurate, and consent to conversation must never be mistaken for consent to interpersonal profiling. "Unmet conversational fit" may turn out to be vague, highly situational, or redundant with existing constructs such as perceived responsiveness and relationship quality. Repeated AI mediation might help some participants, harm others, or make no difference. Results from controlled messaging tasks may not generalize to relationships that develop over weeks or months.

    Authenticity cannot be equated with the absence of tools. Interpreters, accessibility systems, editors, and social norms already mediate human expression. The ethical and empirical questions concern consent, fidelity to intended meaning, transparency, reciprocity, and whether people keep the power to decide. No optimization function should define compatibility or growth on participants' behalf. Before any consequential human studies, the project would need collaborators in communication research, human–computer interaction, and privacy, plus clinical and cultural experts where relevant.

    Project Bridging's proposed contribution is a combination: user-governed conversational discovery, longitudinal where that adds value; transparent interpersonal scaffolding; and explicit assurance against distortion caused by mediation. Its evaluation centers on whether it creates additional opportunities for mutually valued human-to-human connection, not on whether participants spend less time with AI. Continued AI-supported inquiry and meaningful human interaction can coexist, and declining an introduction need not diminish the value of either. The individual components are not claimed to be unprecedented. The framework may be revised, broken apart, or abandoned as evidence develops. Its governing architectural challenge remains:

    Can AI help people find one another without becoming the thing they find instead?
